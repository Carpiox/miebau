Is social media messing up our kids?  A growing number of European countries seem to think it's
causing a problem, as more and more consider bans or restrictions. In an effort to get children to log
off screens and into real life, Spain plans to ban under-16s from social media platforms. For many
adults, it seems to make sense, but how do young people feel about losing access? I've come to
meet a group of 12- to 17-year-olds at Spain's  oldest secondary school in Madrid. Much to my
surprise, all of them support the restriction. "I think that as long as private companies own these
apps, there won't be really positive things because the only thing that matters to them is that you
spend more time in the app for them to make more money," 14-year-old Max explains. Like several
of the other boys in the group, he said he has struggled in the past with social media addiction,
spending hours doomscrolling at the expense of going out with friends. It's a problem that's also
troubled 12-year-old Theo. "I was five or six hours a day watching a screen and I failed my exams,"
he said. "My parents took off all those things [apps] and I started to get good marks." Several of the
girls also have concerns about the impact of social media on their peers' mental health. Elena, 16,
and 17-year-old Vega explain that the algorithms often push endless streams of posts about “clean
eating”, body image and beauty to teenage girls, leading some of them to become trapped in a cycle
of unrealistic comparisons. At times, they say, it can feel overwhelming. "A lot of really young girls
develop eating disorders at a very fast rate, for example, because of social media," adds Vega.
While this group is pro-restrictions, they admit that not all their classmates agree. "We had a debate
in class about this: most girls believe that the boundary is a good idea, while guys think it's not,"
Vega continues.
Social media platforms are made to hack the brain. "Among girls and boys that have
previously been using social media for hours, we see worse mental health," explains psychologist
Silvia Álava. The hormones rushing around teenagers' rapidly developing bodies mean they can
have much stronger reactions to likes or comments on social media than adults. She likens it to the
thrill people get from playing slot machines, and agrees that at such a formative age, children's
brains need to be protected.
The government has also asked prosecutors to investigate potential crimes committed by
Meta, X, and TikTok in connection with the possible creation and dissemination of child porn using
AI. In February, representatives of the Spanish government said that in just 11 days, three million
nude AI-generated images appeared on X, many of them depicting children. In a post a few days
later, X said, "We maintain zero tolerance for child sexual exploitation, including AI-generated
content, and enforce strict policies to keep minors safe and ensure a positive experience for
everyone."
